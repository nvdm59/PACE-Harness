\title{\textbf{PACE: Priced Adaptive Control for Evolving Harnesses}}
\author{Navid Mehrdad}
\date{October 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Agent harnesses contain per-request decisions that nobody prices: which model tier answers, whether a step is verified, whether an answer is refined, which specialist is delegated to. Recent work evolves the harness at design time or trains the orchestrator with an outcome-only reward; we study the complementary runtime problem. The harness is frozen within a generation, and a small, cost-priced controller is learned at its decision points from logged traffic. Three pieces make this deployable: a policy trained offline under a budget whose price is a learned Lagrange multiplier; an off-policy promotion gate that admits a candidate only when doubly-robust estimates with bootstrap intervals clear a quality floor and a cost cap on logged traffic; and priced backward- and forward-transfer metrics for the harness changing underneath the controller. On CRMArena-Pro (300 B2B and 150 B2C tasks, every task on three tiers) the learned policy has a higher priced value than every fixed tier held-out, with higher quality than the frontier tier at 40\% lower cost on B2B; the gate's decision depends on the logged draw, with an interval that narrows as $1/\sqrt{n}$ in the number of logged tasks, and two further suites, $\tau$-bench and EvoHarnessBench's hosted service, reproduce the tier-level picture. A continually trained orchestrator over a growing harness has the lowest regret of every learned arm and bounded, mostly positive transfer. Negative results on multi-agent decomposition, process verification, self-refinement and a language-model controller trained by group-relative policy optimisation (GRPO) are reported as results, each with its cause.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

An agent harness is the scaffold around a model: the loop that decides which tool runs next, which model answers, whether a step is checked, when the episode ends. Inside that loop sit decisions made per request by a rule somebody wrote: ``always use the large model'', ``verify every sub-result'', ``refine if confidence is low''. Each decision has a price in dollars and latency, and each is made without reference to that price. Figure~\ref{fig:overview} shows where these decisions sit and what this paper puts around them.

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
\input{figures/fig_overview.tikz}
\caption{\textbf{PACE.} A frozen harness exposes one decision point per step (which operation, on which model tier). A trace logger records every decision with its propensity and realised cost. A small controller is trained offline from that log under a learned price $\lambda$; an off-policy gate scores each candidate on the logged traffic and promotes or reverts it; drift events (a repricing, a new version, a grown harness) are measured by priced transfer. Within a generation nothing in the model weights or the harness structure moves.}
\label{fig:overview}
\end{figure}

Two lines of recent work optimise the harness rather than the request. The design-time line evolves the harness itself: the Darwin G\"odel Machine \citep{dgm2025}, GEPA \citep{gepa2025}, DarwinX \citep{darwinx2026}, Meta-Harness \citep{metaharness2026}, EnvHarness \citep{envharness2026} and HarnessX \citep{harnessx2026} search over designs, and EvoHarnessBench \citep{evoharnessbench2026} grows a harness $H_t=\{\text{tools},\text{skills},\text{specialist agents}\}$ by stages and measures what a fixed agent policy forgets or gains, reporting $-34.7\%$ backward transfer from agent-pool expansion alone. The orchestrator-training line trains the policy that assembles the system. MAS-Orchestra \citep{masorchestra2026} generates a complete multi-agent system in one step and optimises the generator with an outcome-only reward; it reports that this holistic formulation outperforms stepwise orchestration, in which an orchestrator chooses one tool or model call at a time \citep{toolorchestra2025}, and attributes the gap to long-horizon credit assignment under a single terminal reward. Orchestration reward modelling \citep{rmorchestration2026} then takes the sub-agents out of the training loop by scoring the plan before it is executed, and MAS-ProVe \citep{masprove2026} works at the other end, verifying each agent call or iteration as it happens, and reports that such process verification helps inconsistently and at high variance.

This paper takes the opposite route to the same difficulty. The decisions are per-step and priced, as in the stepwise setting, and control, logging and evaluation are per decision: every served decision logs its propensity (the probability with which it was chosen), the objective is quality minus a learned price of cost, the trainer anchors each of its groups at a logged decision point, and a candidate policy is valued on logged traffic, which it did not generate, by doubly-robust estimators with bootstrap intervals before it is promoted. This does not by itself attribute an outcome to one decision: within a sampled continuation every decision receives the same advantage, and attribution comes from averaging over many groups anchored at different points (Section~\ref{sec:controller}). Holistic generation avoids the credit-assignment problem by removing the steps; the controller here keeps the steps, pays for them with replay and off-policy estimators, and treats per-step verification as one more priced action whose value is estimated rather than assumed. ToolOrchestra's efficiency term is, to our knowledge, the closest prior use of a priced orchestration reward; it is an episode-level reward without a per-decision estimator or a promotion gate, and the stepwise result reported for it by \citet{masorchestra2026} is a transplanted baseline, so the comparison of the two formulations under a common price remains open.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A priced controller} (Section~\ref{sec:method}): a policy over (op, tier) pairs whose objective is quality minus a learned price of cost, trained offline by a constrained group-relative policy gradient on replay (the group-baseline variant of GRPO, without ratio clipping or a reference term), the Lagrange multiplier found by damped dual ascent against a per-task budget and the served policy an iterate average whose budget is measured rather than inherited.
\item \textbf{An off-policy promotion gate}: per-decision inverse-propensity (IPS), self-normalised (SNIPS) and doubly-robust (DR) estimators with cross-fitted direct models and percentile-bootstrap intervals, and a two-bar decision rule on the interval edges. We measure its promotion rate, interval width and empirical coverage against sample size on real agent logs rather than assuming them.
\item \textbf{Priced transfer metrics}: backward and forward transfer in the sense of \citet{lopezpaz2017gem}, applied to the controller's priced objective as the harness is repriced, re-versioned or grown.
\item \textbf{Measurements on four suites} (Section~\ref{sec:results}): 450 CRMArena-Pro tasks on three tiers each, $\tau$-bench retail and airline, three stages of EvoHarnessBench's hosted service, and a 40-day continual-orchestrator study over a frozen, growing harness, together with negative results on decomposition, verification, refinement and LLM controllers, each with its cause stated.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What the measurements say.} On CRMArena-Pro the learned per-category policy has a higher priced value at $\lambda=2$ per dollar than every fixed tier (a cheap, mid or frontier model used for every task) held-out, and on B2B it has higher quality than the frontier tier at 40\% lower cost (0.644 at \$0.079 against 0.614 at \$0.131); on the B2C organisation it matches the frontier tier's quality within 0.002 at 43\% lower cost. The gate's decision on a realised one-arm log depends on the draw, promoting in 30\% of redraws at 300 tasks, and its interval narrows as $1/\sqrt{n}$ in the number of logged tasks, as measured from 150 to 300; resolving a half-cent budget margin is a matter of one to two thousand tasks. On $\tau$-bench and EvoHarnessBench the mid tier matches or beats the frontier tier at about half its cost and the per-task oracle (the best tier for each task in hindsight) beats every tier, while a first-step controller has nothing to condition on in $\tau$-bench. The continual orchestrator has the lowest regret of every learned arm, recovers after every harness stage, and has bounded, mostly positive transfer; a fixed mode beats it over the whole stream because of a twelve-day cold start, the days before the gate's first promotion during which the exploration policy is served. The negative results share one cause: in every experiment the limiting factor was the signal available at the decision point, not the learner. Every number in this paper is taken from the committed result files of the public repository.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/nvdm59/PACE-Harness}}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Cost-aware routing.} FrugalGPT \citep{frugalgpt2023} cascades models by a learned score; RouteLLM \citep{routellm2024} learns a router between a strong and a weak model from preference data, and xRouter \citep{xrouter2025} trains a cost-aware orchestration system with reinforcement learning. These choose a model per query at the first turn and are evaluated by accuracy at a cost. The controller here is a per-step policy with a learned price, served through a gate and evaluated under drift. SkillOrchestra \citep{skillorchestra2026} routes to a typed agent in a pool by a learned competence profile with an explicit cost penalty, learned once from traces; it is a design-time baseline in the continual study (Section~\ref{sec:continual_body}).

\paragraph{Harness evolution and orchestrator training.} The design-time line \citep{dgm2025,gepa2025,darwinx2026,metaharness2026,envharness2026,harnessx2026} and EvoHarnessBench \citep{evoharnessbench2026} evolve or grow the harness. This paper freezes the harness within a generation, learns inside it, and measures what the controller keeps across generations with the transfer definitions of \citet{lopezpaz2017gem}. MAS-Orchestra \citep{masorchestra2026}, orchestration reward modelling \citep{rmorchestration2026}, MAS-ProVe \citep{masprove2026} and ToolOrchestra \citep{toolorchestra2025} are positioned in Section~\ref{sec:intro}; the multi-agent experiment of Appendix~\ref{sec:mas} measures MAS-Orchestra's compute-matched single-agent bar and MAS-ProVe's process-verification finding on one priced log.

\paragraph{Off-policy evaluation and bandits in production.} The Decision Service \citep{agarwal2016decisionservice} and Horizon \citep{gauci2018horizon} established the logging discipline used here: propensities with exploration, and counterfactual evaluation before deployment. The estimators are the per-decision importance sampling of \citet{precup2000eligibility}, self-normalised importance sampling \citep{swaminathan2015snips}, and the doubly-robust estimators of \citet{dudik2011dr} and \citet{jiang2016dr}, with the cross-fitting of \citet{chernozhukov2018dml}. The contribution here is the gate as a deployment rule and the measurement of its power on real agent logs.

\paragraph{Self-evolving agents, test-time compute, and constrained and offline RL.} A position paper from the AReaL group \citep{areal2} names an evolution control plane that chooses among memory, skill, harness and weight updates behind replay-first evaluation; Agent Lightning \citep{agentlightning} exposes the proxy seam that makes per-call logging possible; recursive agent optimisation \citep{rao2026} prices delegation effort. Socratic self-refinement \citep{ssr2025} and test-time scaling are purchases of compute whose value we measure under a budget (Appendix~\ref{sec:ssr}). The trainers draw on constrained RL \citep{stooke2020pid,tessler2018rcpo}, GRPO \citep{shao2024grpo} and DAPO \citep{dapo2025}, and on offline RL \citep{kumar2020cql,kostrikov2021iql,peng2019awr}. Appendix~\ref{app:related} discusses each of these lines at length.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{The harness as a frozen, priced decision process}
\label{sec:problem}

A harness exposes, at each step $t$ of an episode on task $x$, an observable state $s_t$ and a legal set $\mathcal{A}(s_t)$ of actions $a=(\text{op},\text{tier})$: an operation the harness can run (for example continue, answer, retrieve, verify, refine, escalate, delegate, re-plan, call a tool or engage a skill; each host exposes its own set of two to six, listed in Appendix~\ref{app:details}) on a model tier (cheap, mid, frontier; in every experiment here \code{claude-haiku-4-5}, \code{claude-sonnet-5-5} and \code{claude-opus-5-5} at their list prices, Table~\ref{tab:det-collect}). The harness's own transition $s_{t+1}\sim P_H(\cdot\mid s_t,a_t)$ is fixed; the controller $\pi(a\mid s)$ only chooses among what is legal. Each step has a realised cost $c_t$ (tokens times the provider's published per-token price, the list price) and latency $\ell_t$, and the episode ends with a quality $q\in[0,1]$ that is the harness's own score. We write $C=\sum_t c_t$ and $L=\sum_t\ell_t$. The harness is therefore a frozen Markov decision process over the controller's decision points, and the controller is the only thing trained. Figure~\ref{fig:layers} places this inside the two outer layers that do not move: the model weights, frozen throughout, and the harness structure, frozen within a generation (one version of its tools, prompts, agent pool and prices) and changed only between generations.

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
\input{figures/fig_layers.tikz}
\caption{\textbf{Three layers, two loops.} Model weights are frozen. The harness structure $H_t$ (tools, skills, agent pool, prices) is frozen within a generation and changes between generations at design time. The controller is the only layer that learns, in two loops: a serve-time loop (log, train, gate, serve) inside a generation, and a design-time loop across generations, in which priced backward and forward transfer measure what the controller keeps when $H_t$ becomes $H_{t+1}$.}
\label{fig:layers}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Priced objective.} A product specification is a budget, so the objective, which we call the priced value, is
\begin{equation}
J(\pi)=\E_\pi[q]-\lambda\,\E_\pi[C]-\mu\,\E_\pi[L],
\label{eq:J}
\end{equation}
with $\mu=0$ in every experiment reported here (latency is logged but not priced), and the constrained form
\begin{equation}
\max_\pi\ \E_\pi[q]\quad\text{s.t.}\quad \E_\pi[C]\le B,
\qquad
\mathcal{L}(\pi,\lambda)=\E_\pi[q]-\lambda\big(\E_\pi[C]-B\big),\ \lambda\ge0 .
\label{eq:lagrangian}
\end{equation}
The price $\lambda$ is learned by projected dual ascent on an exponential moving average $\hat c$ of realised episode cost, with a proportional damping term (the PID-Lagrangian of \citet{stooke2020pid} without its derivative term; cf.\ RCPO \citep{tessler2018rcpo}):
\begin{equation}
\hat c_k=\rho\,\hat c_{k-1}+(1-\rho)\,\bar C_k,\qquad
\lambda^{\mathrm{int}}_k=\pos{\lambda^{\mathrm{int}}_{k-1}+\eta\,(\hat c_k-B)},\qquad
\lambda_k=\pos{\lambda^{\mathrm{int}}_k+k_p\,(\hat c_k-B)} .
\label{eq:dual}
\end{equation}
Primal--dual iterates orbit the budget rather than converging to it, so the served policy is the statewise iterate average $\bar\pi(a\mid s)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{W\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_W(a\mid s)$ over weight snapshots $\mathcal{S}$ from the second half of training \citep{nedic2009saddle}, and $\bar\lambda$ is the mean price over the same half. The averaging is of action probabilities at each state, not a choice of one snapshot per episode. For a one-decision bandit the two coincide and the mixture's expected quality and cost are the averages of the snapshots'. In a multi-step harness they are not in general: a statewise average can visit state sequences that no snapshot follows, so a budget met by every snapshot is not inherited by their average (Appendix~\ref{app:mixture} gives a two-step counterexample). The budget of the served mixture is therefore measured on the served log, not inferred from training, and the greedy decoding of a mixture is reported as a separate policy with its own measured cost.

\subsection{Controller and trainer}
\label{sec:controller}

\paragraph{Policy class and logged propensities.} The linear controller is a softmax over soft-clipped logits, mixed with $\epsilon$-exploration:
\begin{equation}
z=L\tanh\!\big(Wx/L\big),\qquad
\pi_\epsilon(a\mid x)=(1-\epsilon)\,\mathrm{softmax}_{\mathcal{A}}(z)_a+\frac{\epsilon}{|\mathcal{A}|},
\label{eq:policy}
\end{equation}
where $x=\phi(s)$ is a feature vector and the softmax runs over the legal set. The logit clip keeps the softmax steerable by $\lambda$ and keeps importance ratios bounded. The propensity written to the log is $\mu_t=\pi_\epsilon(a_t\mid x_t)$, exploration included, so every served log is usable for off-policy evaluation, the logging discipline of \citet{bottou2013counterfactual} and \citet{li2011unbiased}. A small language model can take the place of the linear policy (Appendix~\ref{sec:llm}); the logging contract is the same.

\paragraph{Constrained GRPO on replay.} When the harness can be re-rolled from a logged state (a simulator, or a counterfactual lookup table in which every arm of every task is logged), training samples a logged state $s$, draws a group of $G$ continuations from the policy \emph{as served} ($\pi_\epsilon$, so that the cost the dual variable tracks is the served cost), scores each by $R_i=q_i-\lambda C_i$, and uses the group-relative advantage with the group mean as baseline \citep{shao2024grpo}:
\begin{align}
A_i&=R_i-\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^G R_j,\qquad
\nabla_W\mathcal{L}=\sum_i A_i\sum_t\nabla_W\log\pi_\epsilon(a^i_t\mid x^i_t),
\label{eq:grpo}\\
\nabla\log\pi_\epsilon(a\mid x)&=\frac{(1-\epsilon)\,\pi(a\mid x)}{\pi_\epsilon(a\mid x)}\,\nabla\log\pi(a\mid x).
\label{eq:epsscore}
\end{align}
The last factor is the score correction for the $\epsilon$-mixture: the gradient of the mixed policy is the bare softmax's score, scaled by the share of the served probability that the softmax contributes. Two things about this update should be stated plainly. It is the on-policy group-baseline variant of GRPO \citep{shao2024grpo}: no ratio clipping and no reference-policy term, which the language-model backends of Appendix~\ref{sec:llm} do use. And the advantage is continuation-level: every decision in a sampled continuation receives the same $A_i$, so a single group does not say which of its decisions earned the outcome; per-decision attribution emerges only across many groups anchored at different decision points, and the method's per-decision character lies in where groups are anchored and in what is logged and evaluated, not in a per-step reward. The cost of anchoring at decision points is counted in groups: an episode with $T_d$ controller decisions contributes $T_d$ anchors where per-task GRPO would contribute one, with continuations of half an episode on average, and $T_d$ is one to four in every harness here; the continuations run on the simulator or the lookup table and never call a model, which is what makes the trade affordable, and where no re-roll exists the trainer is AWR on the log. Half of the anchors are episode starts and half are interior logged states, so the primal gradient optimises value under that restart distribution rather than under the task-start distribution alone, while the dual step tracks full-episode cost from start-state rollouts; Appendix~\ref{app:restart} compares start-only and mixed anchoring under a matched transition budget. When no replay exists, the trainer is advantage-weighted regression (AWR) with inverse-propensity weights at a fixed price (Appendix~\ref{app:awr}); offline RL can only reweight what the log contains.

\subsection{The off-policy promotion gate}
\label{sec:gate}

\paragraph{Estimators.} The gate must value a candidate from episodes that another policy generated, which is the off-policy evaluation problem. Three estimators are used: inverse propensity scoring (IPS), which reweights each logged outcome by the ratio of the candidate's probability of the logged action to the logging policy's; its self-normalised form (SNIPS), which divides by the realised sum of the weights instead of by $n$, trading a small bias for much lower variance; and the doubly-robust estimator (DR), which starts from a direct model of the outcome fitted on the log and applies the importance weights only to that model's residuals, so that it is correct if either the model or the propensities are. For an episode $i$ with per-step rewards $r^i_t$ (the terminal quality, or the per-step cost) and cumulative ratio $\rho^i_{0:t}=\prod_{k\le t}\pi(a^i_k\mid s^i_k)/\mu^i_k$ clipped at $\rho_{\max}=50$,
\begin{align}
\hat V_{\mathrm{IPS}}&=\frac1n\sum_i\sum_t\rho^i_{0:t}\,r^i_t, \qquad
\hat V_{\mathrm{SNIPS}}=\sum_t\frac{\sum_i\rho^i_{0:t}\,r^i_t}{\sum_i\rho^i_{0:t}},
\label{eq:ips}\\
\hat V_{\mathrm{DR}}&=\frac1n\sum_i\Big[\hat V(s^i_0)+\sum_t\rho^i_{0:t}\big(r^i_t+\hat V(s^i_{t+1})-\hat Q(s^i_t,a^i_t)\big)\Big],\qquad
\hat V(s)=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\,\hat Q(s,a),
\label{eq:dr}
\end{align}
the per-decision importance-sampling estimator of \citet{precup2000eligibility}, the self-normalised estimator of \citet{swaminathan2015snips}, and the sequential doubly-robust (DR) estimator of \citet{jiang2016dr} (see also \citealp{thomas2016magic}); we call Eq.~\eqref{eq:dr}, which carries the cumulative ratio across the steps of an episode, \emph{sequential DR}. The direct model $\hat Q$ is a per-feature-bucket mean fitted from the log and iterated under the candidate, which is bucketed fitted-Q evaluation \citep{le2019batch}; the clip biases DR only through the direct-model residual. On a first-step contextual bandit (one decision per task, fixed continuation) the bandit form of \citet{dudik2011dr}, \emph{bandit DR} below, applies with a \emph{cross-fitted} direct model:
\begin{equation}
\hat V_{\mathrm{DR}}=\frac1n\sum_i\Big[\sum_a\pi(a\mid x_i)\,\hat Q^{(-i)}(x_i,a)+w_i\big(r_i-\hat Q^{(-i)}(x_i,a_i)\big)\Big],\qquad w_i=\frac{\pi(a_i\mid x_i)}{\mu_i},
\label{eq:drbandit}
\end{equation}
where $\hat Q^{(-i)}$ is fitted leaving out task $i$. The cross-fitting matters at small $n$ \citep{chernozhukov2018dml}: an in-sample direct model shares the logged reward it corrects and shrinks the correction toward zero. For multi-step orchestration logs with two to four decisions over twelve actions, a per-decision variant treats each logged decision as a bandit sample with single-step ratio $w_t=\pi(a_t\mid x_t)/\mu_t$ and reward the episode's quality or cost-to-go; it values changing one decision while the rest of the episode stays as logged, trading bias for ratios bounded by $|\mathcal{A}|/\epsilon$. Every estimate is reported with its effective sample size $\mathrm{ESS}=(\sum_i w_i)^2/\sum_i w_i^2$ \citep{kish1965survey}. Appendix~\ref{app:estimators} gives the derivations.

\paragraph{Decision rule.} Confidence intervals are percentile bootstraps over episodes (over tasks, with all logged arms of a task resampled together, in the bandit case), with 200 resamples and the direct model refitted on every resample so that the DR interval includes direct-model variance. The decision is
\begin{equation}
\PROMOTE\iff \hat q^{\,\mathrm{lo}}_{\mathrm{DR}}\ \ge\ q_{\mathrm{prev}}-\epsilon_q
\quad\text{and}\quad
\hat c^{\,\mathrm{hi}}_{\mathrm{DR}}\ \le\ B+\mathrm{tol},
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
with $q_{\mathrm{prev}}$ the incumbent's quality: its on-policy log mean if it is the behaviour policy, its own DR estimate on the log otherwise, or the DR quality recorded at the incumbent's own promotion after a repricing, since an over-budget incumbent would otherwise set a bar that no budget-respecting candidate can pass. Defaults are $\epsilon_q=0.03$ and $\mathrm{tol}=\$0.005$ (0.02 in simulator units). The gate uses the interval edges, not the point estimates. The rule is local: it guards non-inferiority against the current incumbent and a cost cap, not an absolute quality floor, so a sequence of $k$ individually acceptable promotions can drift by up to $k\epsilon_q$ against the first incumbent; and it compares the candidate's lower bound with the incumbent's point estimate rather than bounding their difference. Appendix~\ref{sec:continual} reports a variant that bounds the paired difference and adds an overlap guard.

\subsection{Priced transfer under harness drift}
\label{sec:transfer}

Let $R_{i,j}$ be the held-out priced value $J_j$ of the policy in service at the end of stage $i$, evaluated on stage $j$, with $J_j$ priced at the stage's own $\bar\lambda_j$. Following \citet{lopezpaz2017gem}, backward transfer (BWT) measures what the final policy keeps of its value on an earlier stage and forward transfer (FWT) what the policy in service brings to a stage it has not yet trained on:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{BWT}_j=R_{T,j}-R_{j,j}\ (j<T),\qquad
\mathrm{FWT}_j=R_{j-1,j}-b_j,
\label{eq:bwt}
\end{equation}
with $b_j$ the value of a uniform policy on stage $j$. For the two-version case we report a different quantity and name it differently: the \emph{drift impact} on a frozen policy, $J(\text{frozen on }v_2)-J(\text{frozen on }v_1)$, which measures the environment's change with the policy held fixed, and the recovery, $J(\text{served on }v_2\text{ after retrain and gate})-J(\text{frozen on }v_2)$. BWT in Eq.~\eqref{eq:bwt} compares two policies on one fixed stage and measures what learning kept or lost; the drift impact compares one policy on two stages and would be zero for no policy, so the two must not be read as the same number. The distinction from EvoHarnessBench's metrics is the object measured: there, a fixed agent policy under a changing harness; here, the priced value of a controller that keeps learning while the structure around it changes.

\subsection{Platform}
\label{sec:platform}

A host harness implements \code{reset}, \code{legal\_actions}, \code{step} and \code{quality}; a simulable one also implements \code{snapshot} and \code{restore}, which is what constrained GRPO on replay needs. Its operations are the ops, its model pool the tiers, its version string a feature. The trace schema has one record per controller decision (features, action, propensity with exploration included, the legal set the behaviour chose from, realised cost and latency, and a state snapshot when the harness is simulable) and one record per episode (terminal quality, the agent's answer, the queries, the evaluator's calls). Every model call is logged with its model, token counts, priced cost, task and purpose, and a hard budget check on the call's worst-case cost runs before the call is sent, so every paid collection is fail-closed and resumable per (task, arm). Four hosts are used: a hand-written simulator with twelve actions over a latent task difficulty (Appendix~\ref{sec:sim}); CRMArena-Pro \citep{crmarenapro2025}, whose ReAct agent \citep{yao2023react} is re-expressed as a steppable loop over the benchmark's own tasks, prompts, live customer-relationship-management (CRM) connector and evaluator, with actions \code{CONTINUE@tier} (one more agent turn on that tier) and \code{ANSWER@tier} (force the final answer on that tier) over three tiers and 43 features (Appendix~\ref{app:crm50}); $\tau$-bench \citep{taubench2024}; and the hosted service of EvoHarnessBench \citep{evoharnessbench2026}. Every setting of every run is in Appendix~\ref{app:details}.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:results}

Seven experiments exercise the method in increasing realism. The body reports the four that carry the paper's claims and summarises the rest; every experiment is reported in full, with setup, results and limitations, in the appendices. All real-harness collections follow one \emph{counterfactual protocol}: every task is run on every tier with propensity $1/3$ at the first step and a fixed continuation on the chosen tier, so that the held-out quality and cost of any first-step policy is an exact lookup and the off-policy estimators can be checked against the truth. Costs are token counts times list prices, with one exception stated where it occurs. Two reference quantities recur. The \emph{per-task oracle} picks, for each task, the tier with the best logged quality, cheapest among ties. It is a realised-outcome oracle: it sees the one realised outcome of each arm, so its gap to the best learned policy mixes predictable heterogeneity, which better observables could recover, with hindsight over stochastic outcomes, which nothing could. Three identical arms succeeding independently with probability one half give such an oracle 0.875 against 0.5 for any policy, with no routing signal at all. Appendix~\ref{app:hindsight} bounds the hindsight component from the one repeated run available; the gap is an upper bound on headroom, not a measurement of it. In the continual study the \emph{per-stage oracle} is a controller trained on fresh exploration episodes of that stage with no gate, a reference rather than a proven optimum, and \emph{regret} is the sum over days of the oracle's held-out priced value minus the serving policy's on the day's stage.

\subsection{CRMArena-Pro at 300 B2B and 150 B2C tasks}
\label{sec:crm300}

CRMArena-Pro \citep{crmarenapro2025} poses CRM tasks over a live Salesforce organisation with a program-checked answer, in two sandbox organisations, B2B and B2C, with different schemas and task mixes; the harness is its own ReAct agent, re-expressed as a steppable loop, and the learned decision is the first-step tier from 43 step-0 features, chiefly the task category. The first collection, 50 tasks, is where the controller memorised: no learned policy clearly beat the best fixed tier held-out, and the gate said so (Appendix~\ref{app:crm50}). The collection was then resumed on the same seeded, category-stratified sample to 150 and 300 B2B tasks (900 episodes), and a second collection of 150 tasks was run on the B2C organisation with the same protocol. Everything was re-run with the same code and folds. \subsection{The gate's sample-size result}
\label{sec:gate_body}

The gate is run on the \emph{realised log}, one uniformly drawn tier per task, which is what a deployment log looks like, and then over 200 independent redraws of that log (a fresh uniform draw of one tier per task each time), so that the decision's dependence on the draw is measured rather than assumed. \finding{The gate's answer depends on the draw, and more data moves the rate, not the decision.} On the seed-0 realised log at 300 tasks the DR estimate for the all-task constrained policy is 0.607 [0.525, 0.668] in quality and \$0.095 [0.087, 0.113] in cost, and the gate \REVERT{}s on the cost bound; the policy's exact cost is \$0.098 against a \$0.100 budget, so the candidate sits inside the budget by less than the tolerance and the interval cannot certify it. Over 200 redraws the promotion rate is 30\% at 300 tasks (10\% at 150, 6\% at 50) and 8\% on B2C; the DR cost interval has mean width 0.024 (0.030 on B2C) with 96\% coverage of the exact value, and the DR quality estimate has RMSE 0.029 (Figure~\ref{fig:gatepower}). From 150 to 300 tasks the width fell from 0.036 to 0.024, which is what the $1/\sqrt{n}$ law predicts from the 150-task point (0.025); the projection made from the 50-task width had been optimistic (0.019 at 300), and the promotion rate exceeded the ten to fifteen percent projected from it. Certifying a half-cent margin needs an interval near \$0.01, which from the 300-task width is about 1{,}700 tasks. That number is a result rather than a limitation: it says what a production gate on this kind of log costs in traffic, and it is why Section~\ref{sec:discussion} recommends bounding cost and treating quality as a floor. Two qualifications apply to this experiment. The candidate was trained on the full outcome table and then evaluated on one-arm masks of that same table, so it is a controlled test of the estimators on a fixed table, not an end-to-end test of learning from a sparse log; Appendix~\ref{app:cvgate} adds the end-to-end protocol, with the candidate trained on training folds and gated on the held-out fold's realised log: there DR is unbiased (bias $+0.001$ in quality) and its intervals cover the exact value 98\% of the time, but at 30--60 held-out tasks the quality interval is 0.29--0.40 wide against true gains of 0.06--0.12, so the gate promotes in only 7\% (B2B) and 2\% (B2C) of redraws and the one decision it gets reliably right is reverting the fold whose candidate is over budget. And the redraws are repeated masks of one table, so their coverage is conditional on that table.

\subsection{The continual orchestrator}
\label{sec:continual_body}

The centrepiece asks EvoHarnessBench's question turned around: when the harness $H_t=\{\text{tools},\text{skills},\text{agents}\}$ grows under an orchestrator that keeps learning, what happens to the policy's priced backward and forward transfer, and does the gate keep it safe without making it too slow to matter? The multi-agent structure of Appendix~\ref{sec:mas} is expressed as a typed harness (18 actions over an agent pool, tools and skills; 88 features) on a simulator calibrated to the real logs (Appendix~\ref{app:sim}) and run for 40 days of 96 tasks under four pre-registered stages: a repricing, new specialists including a better verifier, new tools, and skills with a second planner. The continual arm serves its incumbent with $\epsilon=0.1$, trains daily by constrained GRPO on the last five days of replay, and is gated daily against the incumbent by sequential DR, the multi-step form of Eq.~\eqref{eq:dr}, since each episode here holds two to four decisions. Comparators on the same traffic: the frozen day-5 policy, a SkillOrchestra-style router learned once and one re-learned per stage, the fixed modes \code{always\_verify} and \code{never\_verify}, and the per-stage oracle defined above. Appendix~\ref{sec:continual} gives the stages, the day loop, every table, a mild-schedule sensitivity run and the verifier ablation.

\finding{The online learner has the lowest regret of every learned arm and recovers after every stage} (Figure~\ref{fig:regret}): cumulative regret 1.26, against 2.60 for the frozen policy and 3.03--3.46 for the static routers, ending each stage within 0.014 of the oracle. \finding{A fixed mode beats it over the whole stream, and that headline is a cold-start artefact.} \code{never\_verify} has regret 0.56 ($0.74\pm0.08$ against the learner's $1.06\pm0.21$ over 5 seeds): the learner spends twelve days on the uniform exploration policy before its first promotion, crosses the fixed mode around day 22--25 (Figure~\ref{fig:crossover}), and from day 25 leads it (0.22 against 0.50; $+0.031\pm0.007$ at the last stage over 5 seeds), while the fixed mode falls further below the oracle at each capability stage because it cannot use what arrives. \finding{The gate is the conservative element.} 32 of 40 candidates were reverted, 9 rightly (all budget violations) and 23 as false alarms; at $n\approx500$ episodes the DR quality interval is $\pm0.04$ or wider, while the cost interval is \$0.008 wide and its criterion is the one that binds. A paired-difference gate with an overlap guard (Appendix~\ref{app:gateaudit}) halves the learner's regret to $0.70\pm0.11$ over 5 seeds, below the fixed mode, at the price of marginal budget overshoots of at most 0.6 cents per task in 3 of 5 seeds; holding out a gate slice removes the overshoots and most of the power. The served policy is a statewise mixture whose budget is measured, not inherited: its realised cost per task stays within $B+\mathrm{tol}$ in every stage of the committed run, with four of forty days over. \finding{Orchestrator-level BWT is bounded and mostly positive}, because the structure is frozen and only the policy moves; FWT is a property of the policy in service, not of anticipation. \finding{Verification helps only when the verifier is informative and the retry does not come out of the plan's call budget} (Appendix~\ref{sec:ablation}): under the committed configuration \code{always\_verify} is below \code{never\_verify} by $0.033\pm0.001$ in quality; with an informative verifier (one whose flags carry information about correctness, which the measured verifier's do not) and the per-episode cap on role calls raised from 8 to 12, it is above by $0.029\pm0.001$, for $+18\%$ cost. Three follow-ups (Appendix~\ref{sec:followups}) add that RAO-style delegation credit buys over-delegation ($-0.02$ to $-0.04$ in stationary $J$ at every stage), that a meta-learned initialisation halves the learner's regret ($1.06\pm0.21\to0.47\pm0.08$) without changing its first promotion day, and that a skill handbook for the cheap tier does nothing measurable once a no-notes control is run.

\subsection{Negative results, in brief}
\label{sec:negatives}

Three further experiments returned negative results. Each is reported in full in the appendices with its cause; the causes are what make them results.

\paragraph{A GRPO-trained LLM controller did not find the budgeted optimum} (Appendix~\ref{sec:llm}). A 1.7B-parameter model with a low-rank (LoRA) adapter, trained by GRPO on the simulator under the same dual-ascent price, produced near-identical rollout groups (83--97\% of groups degenerate, meaning every completion in the group was the same, which makes the group-relative advantage zero for every value of $\lambda$), so the policy update came only from the few non-degenerate groups and the regularisation terms, while the moving price kept changing which arm those groups favoured; the policy jumped between arms instead of conditioning on the state. An SFT warm start on oracle labels did the conditioning (100\% held-out agreement), and GRPO's contribution was the price, $\lambda\approx0.44$. Platform controls settled the cause: with the price held fixed, TRL, verl on Ray and NeMo-RL all learn the split and agree within 0.001 in priced value, and DAPO's dynamic sampling sharpens it; the moving price, not the sampler or the stack, broke the run.

\paragraph{A planner/worker/verifier decomposition scores below a single agent} (Appendix~\ref{sec:mas}): 0.480 against 0.608 on the same 50 tasks, dominated on the priced frontier; this is MAS-Orchestra's bar, a single agent given the same compute, and it is not cleared. The verifier is an alarm, not a judge, flagging 92\% of wrong and 96\% of correct episodes; an orchestration reward model on process features has an out-of-fold AUROC (area under the ROC curve, 0.5 being chance) of 0.469 against 0.821 for the task category alone.

\paragraph{Priced self-refinement is dominated by buying a better tier up front} (Appendix~\ref{sec:ssr}): the cheap probe that decomposes an answer into claims with self-reported confidences barely predicts a wrong answer (AUROC 0.65), a cheap refinement round breaks as many answers as it fixes, and the mid tier at the first turn scores 0.66 at \$0.115 where the best cheap-refinement policy at that price scores 0.49.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{The signal is the bottleneck.} The same pattern appears in every experiment. On CRMArena-Pro the per-task oracle is far above every arm, but the only observable the controller has at step 0 is the category; on $\tau$-bench it is the domain alone. The orchestration reward model finds no out-of-fold signal in process features while the category has AUROC 0.82; the verifier flags nearly everything; the self-refinement probe barely sees whether the cheap answer is wrong; the LLM controller's GRPO stage had no advantage signal because its rollout groups were identical. In the continual study the gate's quality criterion has too little signal at $n\approx500$ to tell an acceptable candidate from the incumbent, while the cost criterion, whose signal is a sum of logged dollars, works. The learners are not the limiting factor; the observables at the decision point are. Three practical consequences follow. Log richer state at the decision point, which for $\tau$-bench means the conversation so far and for the CRM tasks means the first query's result. Certify cost and treat quality as a floor against regression rather than as an improvement test, because the cost interval closes at a few hundred episodes and the quality interval does not. Keep candidates close to the behaviour policy, since the estimator's bias, not its variance, is what over-stated the one far-from-behaviour arm by $+0.68$, and a local non-inferiority rule cannot stop a chain of small, individually acceptable losses.

\paragraph{When does a learned orchestrator earn its keep?} Only when a harness change moves the right fixed policy. A repricing moves a fixed policy's cost but not its ranking; a new specialist or tool moves the ranking, and only a policy that can see the new capability can use it. If the harness is stable and someone knows the right fixed mode, the learner's whole-stream regret is its cold start, its recovery days and its gate delays, and the fixed mode wins; once the pool has grown past what the fixed mode can use, the learner wins while paying an exploration tax the fixed mode does not. Which fixed mode is right depends on the harness, and that is the argument for learning it: with the measured verifier the right mode is never to verify, with an informative one and a looser cap it is always to verify, and the budgeted learner lands between them under every variant. The meta-learned initialisation of Appendix~\ref{sec:followups} halves the cold start on seen stages and does nothing on the unseen one, which is the shape one would expect from a design-time loop that learns from past generations; Appendix~\ref{app:phaseb} states what a real-traffic continuation would need.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The real-harness evidence is 450 CRMArena-Pro tasks, 109 $\tau$-bench tasks and 150 EvoHarnessBench tasks, each on three tiers, from one seed of each collection; held-out differences between policies are of the order of the across-fold SDs below 300 tasks. The continual study is a simulator calibrated to 130 real multi-agent episodes, with its stage effect sizes assumed; its all-arms run is one seed per schedule and only the ablation and the follow-ups have five. Each GPU run ran once. The same tier on the same tasks moved by 0.12 between two runs a day apart, which bounds what any held-out comparison at this scale can certify. Appendix~\ref{app:limitations} lists every threat to validity we identified, and Appendix~\ref{app:details} every setting of every run.

\section{Simulator pipeline: gate, drift, gated retrain}
\label{sec:sim}

The simulator exercises the whole loop where the truth is known. Tasks have a latent difficulty (easy with probability 0.65, hard 0.35) that the controller never sees; it sees a proxy that is correct 85\% of the time. Twelve actions, $\{\textsc{answer},\textsc{retrieve},\textsc{verify},\textsc{escalate}\}\times\{\text{cheap},\text{mid},\text{frontier}\}$, each have their own cost, latency and quality tables; episodes have at most four steps, with terminal ops forced on the last. Version $v_2$, the drift event, adds \textsc{cached\_retrieve}, near-free evidence that works only on easy tasks; ages the verifier so that \textsc{verify} adds nothing on hard tasks; and reprices the frontier tier by $1.5\times$. Costs are in simulator units.

\paragraph{Setup.} 3{,}000 episodes on $v_1$ under a uniform behaviour policy; constrained GRPO on replay at budget $B=0.30$; the candidate gated against the behaviour log. Then drift: $v_2$ plus a scripted $\mathrm{reprice}(\text{mid},1.2)$; 6{,}000 fresh $v_2$ episodes served by the frozen policy with $\epsilon=0.3$; a retrain warm-started from the frozen policy; the adapted policy gated against the frozen one with the quality bar taken from the frozen policy's $v_1$ promotion. Ground truth is Monte Carlo over 20{,}000 fresh tasks with expected success probability. The whole pipeline is repeated over five replicate seeds (seed $s$ draws every collection, training and bootstrap seed from $s$; Table~\ref{tab:det-sim}); numbers are mean $\pm$ SE over seeds and the per-seed values are in Table~\ref{tab:simseeds}.

\paragraph{Results.} Figure~\ref{fig:sim} shows both gates. On $v_1$ the learned price is $\bar\lambda=0.358\pm0.027$. DR puts the candidate at quality $0.884\pm0.009$ (mean 90\% CI $[0.851,0.910]$) and cost $0.308\pm0.001$ (upper bound $0.311$); the decision is \PROMOTE{} in 5 of 5 seeds, and the ground truth ($0.878\pm0.001$ at $0.306\pm0.002$) lies inside the DR interval in 4 of 5 (in the fifth it is $0.001$ below the lower bound). IPS and SNIPS sit low ($0.760\pm0.022$ and $0.797\pm0.014$): a uniform behaviour over 12 actions and up to 4 steps gives cumulative ratios in the hundreds against a near-deterministic candidate, and the clip at 50 truncates them. After the drift the frozen policy keeps its quality ($0.869\pm0.001$) but its cost rises to $0.413\pm0.003$, over budget, so priced-BWT is $-0.047\pm0.003$ (Table~\ref{tab:simdrift}). The adapted policy is scored at $0.869\pm0.004$ and cost $0.298\pm0.004$ (truth $0.867\pm0.001$ at $0.299\pm0.004$, inside the DR interval in 5 of 5); served, it would recover $+0.039\pm0.003$. The gate promotes it in only 2 of 5 seeds. Every revert fails on quality alone, and all three are false alarms: the bar is the DR quality recorded at the $v_1$ promotion minus $\epsilon_q$, and in those seeds that estimate was high ($0.901$, $0.883$, $0.903$ against truths of $0.877$--$0.878$), so a candidate within $0.01$ of the frozen policy's quality and inside the budget could not clear it. The served recovery is therefore $+0.015\pm0.009$ ($+0.033$ and $+0.042$ where the gate promoted, zero elsewhere). Drift hits cost, not quality: the frozen controller keeps paying for an aged verifier and a repriced frontier, the retrain restores the budget at equal quality, and the quality bar inherited from an optimistic earlier estimate is what keeps it out of service. The continual study finds the same false-alarm mode at larger scale (Appendix~\ref{sec:continual}).

\subsection{The gate's sample-size study at 50 tasks}
\label{sec:gatepower}

\paragraph{Setup.} The candidate is the constrained policy trained on all 50 tasks (exact value: quality $0.665$, cost \$0.0987, within $B+\mathrm{tol}=\$0.105$). The realised behaviour log is one uniformly drawn arm per task (50 episodes). The gate (cross-fitted DR, $\epsilon_q=0.03$) is run once, then over 200 independent redraws of the realised log with a 200-resample bootstrap in each.

\paragraph{Results.} On the single realised log DR estimates quality $0.740$ $[0.627,0.855]$ and cost \$0.0960 $[0.0750,0.1200]$; the quality criterion passes and the cost criterion fails ($0.1200>0.105$): \REVERT. On the full counterfactual log all three estimators reduce to the exact value (the weights over a task's three arms sum to 3) and the interval is pure task-sampling uncertainty from 50 tasks; the cost upper bound is still \$0.1122 and the decision is still \REVERT. Over the 200 redraws (Table~\ref{tab:redraw}) DR halves IPS's quality RMSE ($0.048$ vs $0.094$) with 99\% coverage of its 90\% interval, but its cost interval is \$0.046 wide on average, so the candidate, whose exact cost is \$0.0013 under $B+\mathrm{tol}$, is \PROMOTE{}d in only $12/200=6\%$ of redraws. The gate errs on the safe side; buying power means more tasks or a wider tolerance. With interval width scaling as $1/\sqrt n$, certifying a \$0.005 margin needs an interval near \$0.01, roughly twenty times the tasks, about 1{,}000. Section~\ref{sec:gate_body} reports how this projection fared at 150 and 300 tasks.

\section{LLM controller: GRPO with LoRA on a 1.7B model}
\label{sec:llm}

The linear controller of Section~\ref{sec:controller} can be replaced by a small language model that reads the state as text and emits the action as a tag. This experiment trains such a controller with GRPO under the same learned price and asks whether it finds the budgeted optimum.

\paragraph{Setup.} The controller is Qwen3-1.7B \citep{qwen3} with a LoRA adapter \citep{hu2022lora} ($r=16$ on the attention and MLP projections, bf16), trained with GRPO. The agent models stay frozen; the tiers are the arms. The prompt shows the difficulty proxy, the state, per-tier prices, the budget and the legal actions, and the completion must be exactly one tag \code{<action>TIER OP</action>}; anything else is invalid (reward $-1$, cost booked as the cheapest safe action). After the first action a fixed continuation answers on the same tier; the reward is $q-\lambda C$ with $\lambda$ learned by the same damped dual ascent, once per step from the batch mean cost. Each step generates 64 completions (8 prompts $\times$ 8 generations in phases 1 and 1b; 4 $\times$ 16 in 1c), of length 16, at learning rate $10^{-5}$, over 2{,}000 seeded simulator tasks with the legal-action order shuffled per task (with a fixed order the base model only ever sampled one or two arms). Three runs on the simulator, each run once on one A100-40GB: \code{phase1} ($\lambda$ learning rate 0.1, $k_p$ 2.0, $\beta_{\mathrm{KL}}$ 0.02, 300 steps); \code{phase1b} (a slower dual step: learning rate 0.02, $k_p$ 0.5, EMA 0.95; $\beta_{\mathrm{KL}}$ 0.05; entropy 0.01); \code{phase1c} (150 SFT steps on per-difficulty oracle labels at a price shown in the prompt and drawn from $\{0.0,0.1,\dots,2.0\}$, the adapter merged so that the KL reference is the SFT policy, then 200 GRPO steps with 16 generations, temperature 1.2, $\lambda_0=0.5$). A fourth run (\code{crm\_phase2}) applies the same recipe to CRMArena-Pro fold 0 in lookup mode. Per-run settings are in Appendix~\ref{app:details}.

\paragraph{Results.} Table~\ref{tab:llm} and Figure~\ref{fig:llm}. GRPO alone never found the budgeted optimum, for two reasons that the logs make visible. \emph{Group degeneracy}: within a prompt group the completions are almost always identical, 83.5\% of groups on average in \code{phase1b}, which gives zero group-relative advantage, and it gives zero for every value of $\lambda$, since a common shift of the group's rewards is removed by the group mean. A moving price therefore cannot create a within-group signal; what it does is change the sign of the advantage in the few non-degenerate groups from one step to the next, and, in \code{phase1c}, change the price shown in the prompt and with it the sampled output. The weight updates came from those non-degenerate groups and from the KL and entropy terms, and the policy jumped between near-deterministic arms instead of learning the per-proxy split. \emph{Primal--dual orbit}: in \code{phase1} the policy swings to \code{frontier RETRIEVE} (cost 0.60), $\lambda$ climbs to 1.02 by step 67, the policy collapses onto \code{mid RETRIEVE} (0.155), and $\lambda$ decays to 0 with nothing to pull it back; in \code{phase1b} $\lambda$ is still rising at step 300 (1.37) and the policy has not reached the budget. Mixing is not the bottleneck: a deterministic per-proxy rule reaches $0.814$ at $0.250$ and randomisation adds only $0.021$; what the runs failed to do is condition on the proxy. In \code{phase1c} the SFT stage reached 100\% held-out agreement with the oracle labels (1{,}000 prompts, 0\% invalid) and splits by difficulty where the oracle does; GRPO then left the policy unchanged on all 2{,}000 evaluation tasks (the degenerate-group rate averaged 93\% over steps 1--100 and 97\% over 101--200, even after the rollout temperature was raised from 1.2 to 1.5 at step 20). GRPO's contribution is the price: $\lambda$ settles at $0.44$ (mean $0.443$ over the last 100 steps), which is the slope at which the hard-proxy action flips, and at that price the SFT policy ($J=0.702$) ties the oracle rule ($0.703$) and the LP ($0.702$). No single greedy policy meets $B=0.30$: at any one shown price the policy is a deterministic rule costing $0.33$ or $0.15$; training met the budget by alternating the shown price over time (batch cost $0.301$ and $0.296$ over the two halves), and serving can reach it by drawing the shown price, the price-conditioned analogue of iterate averaging: $0.837$ at $0.305$, derived from the two greedy rules rather than decoded end to end. On CRMArena-Pro fold 0 the SFT policy ignores the shown price (the same per-category rule at every $\lambda$, about \$0.075, under budget) and GRPO again leaves it unchanged.

\paragraph{The platform controls.} The \code{phase1b} failure was reproduced on three stacks, TRL 1.15, verl 0.9.1 on Ray with vLLM, and NeMo-RL, with the same model, adapter, data and dual-ascent settings, and then run again with the price held fixed at $\lambda=0.44$ (Appendix~\ref{app:details}, Table~\ref{tab:det-backends}; \code{results/llm/backends/COMPARISON.md}). With the price moving, every stack reproduces the collapse. With the price fixed, every stack learns the per-proxy split, and the three agree within 0.001 in priced value ($J$ 0.681 / 0.682 / 0.682 at cost 0.333 / 0.334 / 0.333); a fixed-price TRL control without dynamic sampling also splits ($J$ 0.679, within budget), and DAPO's dynamic sampling \citep{dapo2025}, which drops the degenerate groups from the batch, sharpens the hard-task split from 53\% to 98--100\% frontier for $+0.065$ cost. The cause of the failure was the moving price, not the sampler or the platform.

\paragraph{Limitations.} Each GPU run ran once; there is no seed variance. The phase-1 table truncates the linear policy's continuation (its first action is \code{cheap RETRIEVE} because it was trained to keep going; with its own continuation it scored $0.876$ at $0.301$ in the run that produced that file), so its row is not its real performance. Showing $\lambda$ rounded to 0.1 puts $\bar\lambda=0.443$ on the over-budget side of the flip point.

\section{Process-verified multi-agent orchestration}
\label{sec:mas}

This experiment asks whether a planner/worker/verifier decomposition of the CRM task clears the compute-matched single-agent bar that MAS-Orchestra \citep{masorchestra2026} reports, and whether per-step verification of the kind MAS-ProVe \citep{masprove2026} studies carries information on this log.

\paragraph{Setup.} A planner (cheap tier) decomposes a CRM request into 1--3 sub-queries; a data worker (mid) runs one sub-query at a time through the harness's own step path; a verifier (cheap) checks a sub-result against its goal (a structural pre-check fails at \$0 on query errors); the answer is the harness's forced \code{ANSWER@mid}. After every worker step an orchestration policy chooses $\{\textsc{verify},\textsc{skip\_verify},\textsc{replan},\textsc{answer}\}\times$ tier; a fail retries the sub-query once; at most 8 role calls per episode. Fixed modes \code{always\_verify} and \code{never\_verify}, plus a uniform exploration mode with logged propensities, were run on the same 50 B2B tasks as the counterfactual log: 130 episodes (50 + 50 + 30). An orchestration reward model (ORM: logistic regression and a 16-unit MLP over process features) is evaluated out of fold over tasks.

\paragraph{Results.} The headline is negative (Table~\ref{tab:mas}): the decomposition scores below the single mid-tier agent ($0.480$ vs $0.608$) at lower cost and is dominated on the priced frontier at both prices (by mid at $\lambda=2$, by cheap at $\lambda=5$); paired on the 50 tasks the single mid agent wins 11 and loses 5. The plan is fixed up front by a cheap model with no data in hand, while the single agent picks each query after seeing the last result. \code{never\_verify} gives the strong model about 2.7 worker turns and a forced answer, against the single agent's about 5. Under \code{always\_verify}, 47 of 50 episodes hit the 8-call cap, because 65\% of verifier calls fail and each fail spends a retry. Re-weighting each episode's rows by $1/n_{\text{decisions}}$ (the task unit, with total episode cost as the cost estimand) changes the DR outcome lift of that policy from $-0.062$ to $+0.038$, both inside intervals 0.3--0.5 wide (\code{results/mas/perdecision\_units.md}); neither unit supports a quality claim, and the body makes none.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The verifier is a cheap model of the same family checking a mid model; there is no human process label. The 8-call cap interacts with a high-fail verifier, so a higher cap or a better verifier is a different system; the ablation of Appendix~\ref{sec:ablation} varies exactly those two things. Sub-queries run sequentially in one growing context.

\section{Priced Socratic self-refinement}
\label{sec:ssr}

Test-time refinement is a purchase: a probe, some re-solving, a rewrite. This experiment asks whether a priced gate can decide when to buy it, and whether buying it beats buying a better tier at the first turn.

\paragraph{Setup.} One Socratic self-refinement round \citep{ssr2025} on a CRMArena-Pro answer: a cheap probe decomposes the answer into 1--3 claims with self-reported confidences (about \$0.003, so a gate can look before it pays); each claim is re-solved by $k=2$ worker mini-episodes on the chosen tier ($\le3$ turns, with the SOQL run on the live organisation); a rewrite on that tier produces the refined answer; the benchmark's evaluator scores it. The initial answers are the cheap-tier episodes of the counterfactual log (already scored and paid). The run logged a tree, a refinement on every tier for every task, so every policy over $\{\textsc{stop},\textsc{refine}@\text{cheap},\textsc{refine}@\text{mid}\}$ is an exact lookup: \code{never}, \code{always\_1:t}, \code{uncertainty\_gated:$\tau$:t} ($\tau$ swept 0.3--0.9 on the minimum claim confidence), and a softmax-linear priced controller over 36 features trained by exact policy gradient on the tree (budget $B=\$0.10$ with learned $\lambda$, or fixed $\lambda\in\{2,5\}$; 5-fold cross-validation over tasks). Of 42 tasks probed, 41 completed on both tiers. One task is $0.024$ of quality.

\paragraph{Results.} The probe's confidence is weakly informative: AUROC $0.65$ (90\% CI $0.51$--$0.79$, $n=41$) of $1-\min$ claim confidence for predicting a wrong initial answer. The decomposer is pessimistic about everything (minimum claim confidence never above 0.75, below 0.4 on 26 of 41 tasks whether right or wrong), the re-solve signal (AUROC 0.67 cheap, 0.55 mid) is no better than the free one, and 22\% of mid worker samples are ``None''. The comparison that matters is refine-the-cheap-answer against buy-mid-up-front, and buying the tier wins by a wide margin: the single-agent mid arm on the same 41 tasks scores $0.663\pm0.074$ at \$0.115; the best cheap-refinement policy at that price is $-0.174\pm0.085$ paired for $+\$0.004$; \code{always\_1:cheap} is $-0.272\pm0.099$; refining on mid at $2.7\times$ the cost is still $-0.174\pm0.092$; and the frontier tier up front ($0.686$ at \$0.156) beats every refinement policy by $0.17$--$0.30$. The sub-questions need the same schema reasoning that made the cheap agent fail, and the workers inherit it.

\paragraph{Limitations.} One round, 41 tasks, one initial arm; the $\tau$ sweep is tuned on the tasks it is evaluated on. The in-sample controller row shows what 22 category one-hots memorise on 41 tasks. Agreement with $k=2$ is weak, and no sampling parameters are sent.

\section{The continual orchestrator: full results}
\label{sec:continual}

Section~\ref{sec:continual_body} states the findings; this appendix gives the harness, the simulator and its calibration, the pre-registered stages, the day loop and comparators, every result table, the mild-schedule sensitivity run and the verifier ablation.

\paragraph{Harness definition.} The multi-agent structure of Appendix~\ref{sec:mas} is expressed as a typed harness in the terms of SkillOrchestra and EvoHarnessBench: an agent pool of typed specialists (agent id, tier, mode, stage) with modes planner, data-worker, verifier and answer; tools the orchestrator can \code{call@}; skills it can \code{engage@}; and a competence handbook over the 22 task categories, the axis the real run found carries the signal. The harness $H_t=\{\text{tools},\text{skills},\text{agents}\}$ grows monotonically by stages, $H_1\subseteq H_2\subseteq\cdots$, plus a price axis. The structure at each stage is frozen and the \emph{policy} at the decision point is what adapts, so the question is EvoHarnessBench's turned around: when $H$ expands under an orchestrator that keeps learning, what happens to the policy's backward and forward transfer, and does the gate keep it safe without making it too slow to matter? There are eighteen actions, \code{delegate@data-1..4}, \code{verify@verify-1,2}, \code{replan@plan-1..3}, \code{answer@answer-1..4}, \code{call@schema\_lookup}, \code{call@aggregate\_calc}, \code{engage@soql\_aggregate}, \code{engage@date\_window} and \code{engage@decompose\_fine}, and 88 features: the real orchestrator's 66, computed by its own feature function on a view of the simulated state, plus availability flags, tools called, skills engaged, stage and a last-fail flag. Unavailable capabilities have fixed zero feature slots and zero logits, so a policy trained at stage $g$ keeps its weights when stage $g+1$ arrives.

\paragraph{Simulator and calibration.} Days of traffic under several harness changes are beyond what the real collection covers, so the logged process is re-expressed as a seeded simulator whose decision points, legal-set rules and feature layout are the real orchestrator's. Every parameter is either measured from the two committed logs (130 multi-agent episodes, 150 single-agent episodes), with its count, or marked assumed (Appendix~\ref{app:sim}). Measured: the plan-length distribution (4/22/104 of 130), worker step kinds per tier, the retry re-draw, the verifier's per-call fail rates by step kind (72 of 124 on rows, 23 of 24 on empty, 12 of 12 structural at \$0) and its episode-level $P(\text{flag}\mid\text{wrong})=0.92$ and $P(\text{flag}\mid\text{correct})=0.96$, token means per role and tier, per-category accuracy, and the configuration. Assumed: whether a rows step is the \emph{right} rows ($P(\text{good}\mid\text{rows})=0.60/0.75/0.85$ by tier), the logit slope from plan coverage to the answer (1.5, centred so that \code{never\_verify} reproduces the category base rates), a task-level logit noise (sd 0.4), and everything that arrives after stage 0. On the stage-0 harness the two fixed modes reproduce the real run's process numbers (Table~\ref{tab:calib}).

\paragraph{The day loop and comparators.} Each day is 96 seeded tasks, the same for every arm. The continual arm serves its incumbent with $\epsilon=0.1$ and logs propensities; a learner trains every day by constrained GRPO on replay from the last 5 days of logged states (60 iterations, batch 16, group 6; $\lambda$ learned against $B=\$0.090$ per task; iterate averaging), warm-started from itself; the gate (sequential DR with 200 bootstrap resamples, $\epsilon_q=0.05$, $\mathrm{tol}=\$0.005$) scores the learner against the incumbent on the log served since the last harness event (up to 5 days), with the budget net of the policy-independent prefix (planner plus first worker call, \$0.022 at list prices). An AWR+IPS variant (fixed $\lambda=2$, 200 epochs) runs the same loop. Baselines on the same traffic: \code{frozen} (the learner as of day 5, served without $\epsilon$, restricted to stage-0 actions); \code{so\_static}, a SkillOrchestra-style router whose handbook is the literal rule $\mathrm{competence}[(a,\text{category})]-\lambda_c\cdot\overline{\mathrm{cost}}[a]$ with cost tie-break, learned from the warm-up exploration traces (days 1--4) with $\lambda_c$ chosen on day 5 by episode-level SN-IPS and never updated (new capabilities scored at the handbook's prior); \code{so\_relearn}, the same router re-fitted after every stage (3 days served with $\epsilon=0.3$, then a refit and re-tune, the lag counted); \code{always\_verify}; \code{never\_verify}; and a per-stage \code{oracle} (constrained GRPO on 200 fresh exploration episodes of that stage, 300 iterations, no gate: a reference, not a proven optimum). Held-out evaluation uses 150 fixed tasks per stage, scored by simulator expected quality and never seen by the gate; $J_g=\text{quality}-\bar\lambda_g\cdot\text{cost}$ with $\bar\lambda_g$ the stage oracle's learned price (1.25 / 0.85 / 0.81 / 0.66 / 0.73 per dollar for g0--g4), at which the budgeted optimum maximises the Lagrangian, so that $J_g$ has a ceiling and regret is well defined. Metrics follow Eq.~\eqref{eq:bwt}; in addition, hit$_j$ is the event-day policy's $J$ on stage $j$ minus on stage $j-1$, recovery$_j=R_{j,j}-R_{j-1,j}$, days-to-recover is the first day of the stage within 0.02 of the oracle, stationary $J$ the mean over the stage's last four days, and cumulative regret the sum over days 6--40 of the oracle's $J$ minus the serving policy's on the day's stage. One seed; five seeds for the arms covered by the ablation below.

\emph{(1) The fixed mode \code{never\_verify} wins the whole stream; against the learned alternatives the continual orchestrator wins on every metric.} Cumulative regret over days 6--40 (Table~\ref{tab:stream}, Figure~\ref{fig:regret}): \code{never\_verify} $0.56$, continual (constrained GRPO on replay, written cgrpo in the tables; gated) $1.26$, \code{always\_verify} $1.99$, frozen $2.60$, router re-learned per stage $3.03$, static router $3.46$, continual AWR $3.90$. \code{never\_verify}, the all-mid fixed mode, is at the oracle on g1 ($0.466$ vs $0.460$) and within $0.02$--$0.04$ on g2--g4 without learning anything: after the mid repricing, running every sub-query and the answer on the half-price mid tier is the budgeted optimum or near it in this simulator. \emph{(4) The AWR+IPS variant is the cautionary row, and the caution is about the estimator and the local rule, not about a recorded false promotion.} It was promoted 14 times and is the worst learned arm (stationary $J$ $0.32$--$0.36$). The gate's sequential DR over-estimated its quality by $+0.68$ on average (coverage 6\% against the held-out reference). Yet an audit of the stored gate records (Appendix~\ref{app:gateaudit}) finds that none of its 14 promotions violated the gate's own rule under the reference: each promoted candidate was within $\epsilon_q$ of its incumbent and within budget. The arm's poor aggregate value is what a local non-inferiority rule permits, a chain of individually acceptable candidates that drift by up to $\epsilon_q$ each against a weak starting incumbent, compounded by estimates that could not have stopped a bad one. AWR's exponentiated inverse-propensity weights move the policy far from the behaviour in one full-batch step; the cumulative ratios (clipped at 50) then carry the estimate with a per-decision ESS of 25\% and a bias the clip does not remove. The per-decision estimators' bias on the same candidates is $+0.18$, better but not a safe bar. The cgrpo candidates, trained on rollouts sampled from the $\epsilon$-mixed served policy, stay close to the behaviour (ESS 54\%) and the same estimator is nearly unbiased on them ($-0.005$). The gate is only as good as the overlap between candidate and behaviour, and a trainer that preserves that overlap is part of the safety story. One confound should be named: the AWR arm's logits saturate the soft clip (Appendix~\ref{app:gateaudit}), so its deficit mixes the update rule with a loss of plasticity, and under the paired-difference gate with an overlap guard it is simply declared unevaluable on most days.

\emph{(5) Orchestrator-level BWT is bounded and mostly positive.} Figure~\ref{fig:bwt}. The continual arm's BWT per stage is $+0.111$ (g0), $+0.023$ (g1), $+0.009$ (g2), $-0.019$ (g3); the mild schedule gives $+0.120$, $+0.026$, $+0.002$, $-0.009$. Later training did not undo earlier behaviour, and the growing harness makes that easier but does not guarantee it: a capability that arrives at stage $g$ has a zero feature slot and a zero logit before it, but later updates still move the shared weights of the old actions and old features, and those updated weights are what runs on the old stages (a unit test in the repository shows that masking new actions does not freeze old behaviour). Positive BWT here is a measurement, not a consequence of monotone growth. Contrast EvoHarnessBench's $-34.7\%$ BWT for agent-pool expansion under a fixed policy: here the pool expansion costs the fixed policies at the event (frozen hit $-0.041$, static $-0.033$ at g2) and they never recover (stationary $J$ falls from $0.411$ / $0.385$ at g1 to $0.350$ / $0.316$ at g4), while the policy that moves loses nothing on the old stages.

\emph{(6) FWT is a property of the policy in service, not of anticipation.} New capabilities arrive with zero logits, so the event-day policy is the previous stage's; the continual arm's FWT on g2--g4 ($+0.085$ / $+0.130$ / $+0.128$) exceeds the frozen arm's ($+0.046$ / $+0.057$ / $+0.032$) because it is a better policy, and the static router's FWT falls to $-0.002$ at g4 because its handbook prices unseen capabilities at a prior. Hits at the events are small for every arm except the price event, where the halved mid price lifts every mid-heavy policy by $+0.05$ to $+0.10$.

\emph{(7) The SkillOrchestra-style router is the weakest design-time baseline here, and the reason is diagnostic.} The competence of an action is the mean outcome of episodes in which it was taken, which under per-step exploration is confounded by everything else in the episode: the handbook over-selects \code{verify@verify-1} (28\% of decisions), an action the real run showed buys nothing. And $\lambda_c$ is tuned by episode-level SN-IPS of a deterministic router against $\epsilon=0.1$ traffic, whose ESS is 1--8 of 96, so the choice of $\lambda_c$ is noise and the re-learned router's $\lambda_c$ swings between 0 and 20 across stages. Re-learning per stage recovers some of the agent-pool loss ($+0.043$ at g4) at the price of three exploration days per stage, and still ends $0.07$--$0.11$ below the oracle. This is a statement about applying the competence-minus-priced-cost rule to multi-step orchestration traces with a per-step router, not about SkillOrchestra's own handbook learning from richer per-skill traces on its own benchmarks.

\paragraph{Mild-schedule sensitivity.} Figure~\ref{fig:mild} and Table~\ref{tab:stream}: the ordering is unchanged with smaller gaps (continual $1.10$, \code{never\_verify} $0.39$, frozen $1.97$, static $2.90$). In the mild run a learner exceeds the oracle at g1 (gap $+0.006$), a reminder that the oracle is a reference, not a proven optimum.

\section{Three follow-up experiments: delegation credit, meta-learned initialisation, and a skill handbook}
\label{sec:followups}

Each follow-up changes one thing in the continual study or the first collection and is run over five seeds; each has the control that its result needed.

\paragraph{RAO-style delegation credit (negative).} We added the node reward of \citet{rao2026} to the continual orchestrator's trainer: for a \texttt{delegate} decision, the per-decision reward is the episode's priced outcome plus $\lambda_d$ times the mean success of the sub-call it spawned, with a leave-one-out baseline anchored on the root reward. Over five seeds on the committed simulator and schedule, the delegation share rose at every stage (from 0.17/0.44/0.50/0.51/0.55 across the five stages without credit to 0.26/0.47/0.57/0.62/0.62 with $\lambda_d{=}0.5$) and served regret over days 6--40 rose from $0.94\pm0.27$ to $1.74\pm0.18$; stationary priced $J$ fell by 0.02--0.04 at every stage after the baseline. Because the credit mode also changes the baseline, we ran the same mode with $\lambda_d{=}0$: regret $0.99\pm0.24$ and unchanged shares. The credit, not the baseline, buys over-delegation. The learner without credit already delegated to new specialists from the stage they appeared; a bonus outside the baseline turns that into a habit.

\paragraph{Meta-learned controller initialisation (positive, caveated).} The learner's deficit against the fixed policy was its cold start. Following the meta-learned initialisation of \citet{tandon2025e2ettt}, we learned the controller's initial weights by a first-order meta-loop over the simulator's stage definitions $g_0$--$g_3$ (never $g_4$, never a held-out set; audited per run), then ran the continual arm from that initialisation. Over five seeds, served regret over days 6--40 fell from $1.06\pm0.21$ to $0.47\pm0.08$ (paired $-0.59\pm0.15$) and over days 17--40 from $0.76\pm0.18$ to $0.43\pm0.07$; the learner now has lower regret than \texttt{never\_verify} ($0.74\pm0.08$) on the full stream. The gain comes from what is served before and between promotions, not from earlier promotion (the first-promotion day is unchanged at $5.2$ in this arm set), and there is no gain on $g_4$, the one stage the meta-initialisation never saw. The caveat is structural: the initialisation was trained on the simulator's own stage definitions; on real traffic it would have to be learned from past harness generations, which is the design-time loop of Figure~\ref{fig:layers}.

\paragraph{Skill handbook for the cheap tier (null, with the control that showed it).} The cheap tier scores 0.444 on the first 50 real tasks because it fails schema reasoning, while the mid and frontier tiers solve many of the same tasks. We distilled per-category procedural notes from those successful SOQL episodes with the mid-tier model as writer (leave-one-task-out, so no task saw notes from its own solution; 49 writer calls) and served the cheap tier with the notes in its prompt. Quality rose to 0.581 ($+0.137\pm0.070$ paired). But the 14 tasks that received no notes improved by the same amount as the 36 that did, so we re-ran the cheap tier with no notes: 0.561, a difference from the skilled arm of $+0.020\pm0.067$. The handbook did nothing measurable; the original 0.444 was a low draw. What survives is the size of that draw: the same tier on the same 50 tasks moved by $+0.117\pm0.062$ between two runs a day apart, which is as large as the gap between tiers in Appendix~\ref{sec:crm}, and a second, independent reason the gate of Appendix~\ref{sec:gatepower} refused to certify a margin of that size.

\section{Advantage-weighted regression with inverse propensities}
\label{app:awr}

When no replay exists, so that a logged state cannot be re-rolled, the trainer is advantage-weighted regression \citep{peng2019awr} on logged steps with return $R_i=q_i-\lambda\,C^{\to}_i$ (cost-to-go from the step), a per-bucket value baseline $\hat V$, and weights corrected by the logged propensity and self-normalised over the batch:
\begin{equation}
A_i=R_i-\hat V(b(x_i)),\qquad
w_i=\frac{\exp(A_i/\beta)/\mu_i}{\sum_j\exp(A_j/\beta)/\mu_j},\qquad
\max_W\ \sum_i w_i\log\pi(a_i\mid x_i).
\label{eq:awr}
\end{equation}
Here $\lambda$ is a fixed price. Logged counterfactual arms, when present, are added as extra records; offline RL can only reweight what the log contains. The AWR arm appears in the first CRMArena-Pro collection (Appendix~\ref{sec:crm}), the 300-task results (Section~\ref{sec:crm300}), the $\tau$-bench tables (Appendix~\ref{sec:suites}) and the continual study, where it is the cautionary row: with the largest distance from the behaviour policy (effective sample size 25\% against 54\% for the replay trainer) it is the arm whose quality the gate's estimator over-stated most. The weights deserve one more caveat: $1/\mu_i$ corrects the action propensity at the logged step, not the distribution of states the behaviour policy visited nor the behaviour continuation inside the logged return, and exponentiating a noisy return is not the same as exponentiating its expectation. The objective is therefore a propensity-corrected imitation of high-return logged actions, useful as a logs-only trainer, and not an unbiased optimiser of the candidate's full-policy value.

\section{What a real-traffic continuation of the continual study would need}
\label{app:phaseb}
\label{sec:phaseb}

The same loop on the real orchestrator: days of 20 B2B tasks, the learner trained with AWR+IPS on the day's log (no replay exists on the real harness) and gated with the per-decision estimators, held-out evaluation by re-serving a fixed 20-task set per stage. Stages that can be induced for real: a repricing (free, with costs recomputed from logged tokens); a better verifier (swap its prompt or tier); a new worker or answer backbone (any priced model); a worker swap by tier; a schema-lookup tool (one describe call) or a local aggregation tool; skills as steering text (tokens only). Such a run needs about 500 served episodes per gate for the cost criterion to bind, as the simulator study found, a quality bar on the paired difference rather than the incumbent's point estimate, a stated label contract (which outcome counts as $q$, when it arrives, and how missing labels are treated, since a missing label is neither a success nor a failure), and a separate ledger for exploration, training, evaluation and failed runs, so that the traffic needed to amortise adaptation is a number rather than an assumption. The gate's quality criterion will not certify improvements at that size; it will only reject regressions, which is the use the discussion recommends.

\section{Extended related work}
\label{app:related}

\paragraph{Cost-aware routing.} FrugalGPT \citep{frugalgpt2023} cascades models by a learned score to cut cost; RouteLLM \citep{routellm2024} learns a router between a strong and a weak model from preference data; xRouter \citep{xrouter2025} trains a cost-aware orchestration system with reinforcement learning. These choose a model per query at the first turn. This paper prices every decision point inside a frozen harness, learns the price as a dual variable against a budget, and gates deployment off-policy. SkillOrchestra \citep{skillorchestra2026} routes each query to a typed agent in a pool by a learned competence profile with an explicit cost penalty, learned once from traces and then served statically; the continual study implements its literal routing rule as a baseline and finds, in finding (7) of Appendix~\ref{sec:continual}, why a competence learned from per-step exploration traces of a multi-step orchestrator is confounded.

\paragraph{Self-evolving agent systems and the deployment seam.} A position paper from the AReaL group \citep{areal2} argues that enterprise self-evolving agents are blocked not by RL algorithms but by systems: a step-level episode-log protocol, a data proxy that turns production workloads into governed learning material, and a unified \emph{evolution control plane} that selects, from log statistics, drift indicators and cost, among memory updates, skill updates, harness edits, weight updates, rollback or no-op, each behind replay-first evaluation, regression tests and canary rollout. This paper instantiates part of that architecture: the gate is a measured, interval-valued form of their replay-first check; priced backward and forward transfer are the drift indicators they call for; and the continual orchestrator is a per-request learner inside the harness surface they name, with the harness-edit surface left to the slow loop. Their prototype realises the weight-update path by redirecting deployed agents' inference calls through managed workers, the same seam that Agent Lightning \citep{agentlightning} uses to make training harness-agnostic through a proxy that emulates the inference APIs. Placing the controller in that proxy is the deployment path we would take, since tier choice, verification and stopping are decisions at the API boundary, while re-planning and delegation still need a hook inside the orchestrator. Production precedent for the cadence exists: online RL after deployment has shipped with checkpoints refreshed from user interaction every few hours.

\paragraph{Recursive delegation and priced effort.} Recursive Agent Optimization \citep{rao2026} trains agents that spawn instances of themselves through a \texttt{launch\_subagent} call, with a local node reward equal to the node's own success plus $\lambda_d$ times the \emph{mean} success of its children (averaging rather than counting, which removes the incentive to spawn), a leave-one-out baseline anchored on the root reward, and depth-level inverse-frequency weighting so that deep sub-tasks do not dominate the update. Four points carry over. That node reward is the principled process reward for the delegate action of Appendix~\ref{sec:mas}, where our process rewards were definitional; the follow-up of Appendix~\ref{sec:followups} adopts its form and finds that it buys over-delegation under a price. Depth weighting is the recursive counterpart of the per-decision estimators used here. Delegation in RAO is unpriced, with depth and step caps standing in for a budget, so adding $\lambda\cdot\mathrm{cost}$ and $\mu\cdot\mathrm{latency}$ to the node reward is the one-line extension that turns the caps into a learned price. And RAO's generalisation from medium to hard tasks concerns the learned decomposition skill, not drift of the harness around it, which is what the transfer metrics here measure. On the shaping of the price itself, the Composer~2 report describes a nonlinear effort penalty over tokens, tool calls and turns whose slope is steeper on easy tasks than on long-horizon ones; the objective here uses a single linear $\lambda$, and a difficulty-conditioned price, $\lambda$ per task category or a concave cost curve, is a direct extension that the logs here are too small to fit.

\paragraph{Contextual bandits in production.} The Decision Service \citep{agarwal2016decisionservice} and Horizon \citep{gauci2018horizon} established the logging discipline (propensities with exploration), the offline evaluation \citep{li2011unbiased,bottou2013counterfactual} and the promotion workflow that the gate follows; the estimators are those of \citet{dudik2011dr,jiang2016dr,thomas2016magic,swaminathan2015snips}, with cross-fitting after \citet{chernozhukov2018dml}.

\paragraph{Test-time compute and self-refinement.} Socratic Self-Refine \citep{ssr2025} decomposes an answer into verifiable sub-questions and rewrites where confidence is low; the uncertainty signals (verbalised confidence, sample agreement) follow \citet{verbalizedconf2025}. Appendix~\ref{sec:ssr} treats how much of it to buy per request as a priced decision and finds that, on a CRM benchmark, a better tier up front dominates.

\paragraph{Process verification and reward modelling for multi-agent systems.} MAS-ProVe \citep{masprove2026} verifies multi-agent executions at the level of the individual agent call or of one iteration, online, by sampling three candidates and keeping the one the verifier ranks highest, across six frameworks and five benchmarks; it finds the gains inconsistent and high-variance, generative judges ahead of reward models, and step-only context as good as the full history. Its unit of verification is the decision point studied here, and its finding is one this paper reproduces on a priced log: the verification itself is a purchased action, three calls for one, and whether it pays is a per-decision question. Reward modelling for orchestration \citep{rmorchestration2026} scores an orchestration plan before any sub-agent runs, with a Bradley--Terry model trained on preference pairs mined from execution logs, so that best-of-$N$ selection and orchestrator training avoid full rollouts; it is a reward model, not an estimator, and the natural combination with the gate is as the direct model inside DR. MAS-Orchestra \citep{masorchestra2026} trains a holistic orchestrator with an outcome-only reward and asks when a multi-agent system beats a compute-matched single agent. ToolOrchestra \citep{toolorchestra2025} trains a stepwise orchestrator over tools and models with a composite reward that includes an efficiency term, the closest prior use of a priced orchestration reward, though unpriced at the per-decision level and without an off-policy gate. Appendix~\ref{sec:mas} measures the first three on one log and finds the verifier uninformative, the process features without out-of-fold signal, and the single agent ahead.

\paragraph{Harness evolution and continual-learning metrics.} The design-time line \citep{dgm2025,gepa2025,darwinx2026,metaharness2026,envharness2026,harnessx2026} and EvoHarnessBench \citep{evoharnessbench2026} evolve or grow the harness; the BWT and FWT definitions are those of \citet{lopezpaz2017gem}, applied here to a priced objective at the orchestrator level. Meta-Harness in particular proposes a new harness by reading the full execution history of the previous ones; it is the natural producer of $H_{t+1}$ in the design-time loop of Figure~\ref{fig:layers}, and the gate here is what would decide whether a controller trained on $H_t$ still serves under it.

\paragraph{Offline and constrained RL.} CQL \citep{kumar2020cql} and IQL \citep{kostrikov2021iql} are the standard pessimistic offline learners; AWR \citep{peng2019awr} is the simpler weighted-regression trainer used here with inverse propensities; GRPO \citep{shao2024grpo} supplies the group-relative advantage and DAPO \citep{dapo2025} the dynamic-sampling and clipping variants used in the platform controls; the learned price follows RCPO \citep{tessler2018rcpo} and the PID-Lagrangian \citep{stooke2020pid}, with iterate averaging after \citet{nedic2009saddle}.

\section{Limitations and threats to validity, in full}
\label{app:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Sample sizes.} The real-harness evidence is 300 B2B and 150 B2C tasks on CRMArena-Pro, 60 and 49 tasks on the two $\tau$-bench domains, and 50 tasks at each of three EvoHarnessBench stages, every task on every tier. The held-out numbers of Appendix~\ref{sec:crm} are means over 10 tasks per fold, where one task is $0.1$ of quality and the across-fold SDs are as large as the differences between policies; the 300-task section is where the orderings become stable, and the gate's intervals are still five times the budget margin there. The multi-agent run has 130 episodes on the first 50 tasks; the self-refinement run 41 tasks. The negative results for the ORM, the refinement gate and the first-step policies on $\tau$-bench are statements about these sample sizes and observables, not about the approaches.
\item \textbf{Seeds.} The simulator pipeline reports 5 seeds; the CRMArena folds one split, and each GPU run ran once; the continual study's all-arms run is one seed per schedule, and only the verifier ablation and the follow-ups (continual, the two fixed modes and the oracle) report 5-seed error bars. The router and AWR arms carry no seed variance.
\item \textbf{Simulators calibrated to the project's own logs.} The simulator of Appendix~\ref{sec:sim} is hand-written. The continual simulator's process statistics are measured on 130 episodes, but the link from a step's latent quality to the answer and every capability that arrives after stage 0 are assumed (Appendix~\ref{app:sim}); the transfer effect sizes are the simulator's, not CRMArena's. The calibration check reproduces the fixed modes' cost and call counts but lands accuracy at $0.446/0.459$ against $0.480/0.460$, because the simulated verifier is uninformative, as the measured one is.
\item \textbf{Assumed stage parameters.} $P(\text{good}\mid\text{rows})=0.60/0.75/0.85$ by tier; coverage-to-answer logit slope 1.5; task-level logit noise sd 0.4; \code{verify-2} at $P(\text{flag}\mid\text{correct})=0.30$ with the same recall; \code{data-4} at rows 0.85 / good 0.80; \code{answer-4} mid-grade on 8 categories; \code{schema\_lookup} removes error mass from the next step; \code{aggregate\_calc} adds $+0.34$ coverage on aggregation categories; the three skills add $+0.15$ $P(\text{good}\mid\text{rows})$ on their categories or re-plan into 3--4 sub-queries; planner v2 plan length $(0.0,0.1,0.4,0.5)$. Tools and skills are priced as a verifier-sized cheap call (711/62 tokens).
\item \textbf{The SkillOrchestra baseline is the literal rule, not their system.} Competence minus $\lambda_c$ times mean logged cost, over task categories as skills, learned from per-step exploration traces of a multi-step orchestrator. Their handbook learning from richer per-skill traces on their own benchmarks is not reproduced.
\item \textbf{The oracle is a reference.} Constrained GRPO on fresh exploration with more iterations, not a proven optimum; regret can be slightly negative.
\item \textbf{Gate design.} The simulator gate fits its direct model in-sample; the bandit gate cross-fits by task; the continual gate uses the sequential DR with cumulative ratios clipped at 50, whose bias on far-from-behaviour candidates is what finding (4) of Appendix~\ref{sec:continual} exposes.
\item \textbf{The $\tau$-bench first-step policies.} With the domain one-hot as the only step-0 feature, the learned first-step policies cannot differ from a fixed arm; the suite bounds the headroom and says what to log next, and nothing more.
\item \textbf{EvoHarnessBench pricing and coverage.} Its cost column is at cached prices; stages 3, 5 and 6 and the backward-transfer cells were not collected; no controller was trained on its logs.
\item \textbf{Run-to-run variation of a tier.} The same tier on the same 50 tasks moved by $+0.117\pm0.062$ between two runs a day apart (Appendix~\ref{sec:followups}), which bounds what any held-out comparison at that scale can certify.
\item \textbf{Fold SD and exact lookups.} The across-fold SD describes the spread of five overlapping training sets and is not a confidence interval; a lookup is exact for the stored outcome table and removes none of the task-sampling, outcome-sampling or run-to-run variation. No number in this paper treats folds or redraws of one table as independent repetitions of the whole procedure.
\item \textbf{Budget semantics.} $B$ is an expected per-task cost, not a per-request cap or a latency guarantee; exploration imposes a cost floor over the legal set, so some budgets are infeasible under a mandated $\epsilon$, and an increasing multiplier cannot manufacture a feasible policy. The collection spend cap protects the experiment, not a request.
\item \textbf{Baseline confounds in the continual study.} The frozen arm is restricted to stage-0 actions, so its deficit combines missing capabilities with absent learning; the per-stage reference policy receives fresh exploration and more optimisation; the lookup trainer sees every arm where a production log would not. Each comparison isolates one factor only where stated.
\item \textbf{Quality labels in deployment.} Every quality here is a benchmark evaluator's. On real traffic quality is delayed, sparse or a judge's proxy; logging propensities does not supply labels, and the gate's quality criterion depends on their meaning and availability.
\item \textbf{Data terms.} CRMArena-Pro is CC BY-NC 4.0 (research use), fetched at setup and not copied; the committed traces are derived research artefacts under the same terms (Appendix~\ref{app:license}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Additional analyses prompted by review}
\label{app:review}

\subsection{Mixture semantics and the budget of the served policy}
\label{app:mixture}

The served policy is the statewise average of weight snapshots, $\bar\pi(a\mid s)=\frac1{|\mathcal S|}\sum_W\pi_W(a\mid s)$. For a one-decision bandit the expected quality and cost of $\bar\pi$ are the averages over snapshots, because the context is exogenous. In a multi-step harness this fails in general. A two-step counterexample, implemented as a regression test in the repository: the first action chooses a branch at zero cost; at the second step the action that matches the branch costs 1 and the other costs 9. One snapshot prefers action $a$ everywhere and the other prefers $b$ everywhere ($W_2=-W_1$, logit clip 3, $\epsilon=0.1$). Each snapshot's expected episode cost is 1.79, as is a mixture that picks one snapshot per episode; the statewise average costs 5.00, because it takes the branch of one snapshot and the second action of the other. A budget of 2 is met by both snapshots and violated by their average. The iterate-averaging argument from convex optimisation \citep{nedic2009saddle} therefore does not establish the budget for this implementation, and the paper does not rely on it: the served mixture's cost is measured on the served log. For the continual study, the measured mean cost per task of the served policy by stage, against $B+\mathrm{tol}$, is given in Appendix~\ref{app:gateaudit}. Changing the mixing semantics to an episode-level choice of snapshot would require logging the latent snapshot choice, since the trajectory probability under the mixture is no longer a product of per-state mixture probabilities.

\subsection{Start-only against mixed anchoring of the replay trainer}
\label{app:restart}

The replay trainer draws half of each batch's anchors from episode starts and half from all logged states, with the restart distribution held fixed within an update. Its primal step therefore ascends $F_\rho=(1-\rho)\,\E_{s\sim d_0}[V_\pi(s)]+\rho\,\E_{s\sim d_L}[V_\pi(s)]$, where $d_0$ is the task-start distribution and $d_L$ the distribution of logged states, while the dual step tracks full-episode cost from start-state rollouts; the two Lagrangians coincide only if the policy class can be optimal at every state at once. At $\rho=0.5$ about 77\% of the restart mass falls on episode starts, because logged states include the starts. To measure what the mixing does, the simulator pipeline was run with $\rho=0.5$ (the committed default) and $\rho=0$ under a matched budget of simulator transitions (start-only training consumes about 8\% more transitions per iteration, so it ran 281 iterations against 300), over seeds 0--4, with ground truth over 20{,}000 fresh tasks (\code{scripts/restart\_ablation.py}).

\subsection{The hindsight component of the realised-outcome oracle}
\label{app:hindsight}

The per-task oracle selects the best realised arm, so part of its gap to any policy is luck over stochastic outcomes rather than predictable heterogeneity. One repeated run exists: the cheap tier was run twice on the first 50 B2B tasks, a day apart (Appendix~\ref{sec:followups}). Correctness agrees between the two runs on 80\% of tasks (10 flips: 8 wrong-to-right, 2 right-to-wrong; accuracy 0.44 then 0.56, so drift between runs cannot be excluded), the mean per-task outcome variance is 0.10, and the test--retest correlation is 0.62 (90\% bootstrap interval 0.45--0.79). On these 50 tasks the realised oracle scores 0.76 against 0.66 for the best single tier. Under a mean-preserving shrinkage model that borrows the cheap tier's repeatability for the mid and frontier tiers, whose noise was not measured, the expected probability oracle is 0.72--0.74, so the hindsight component is about 0.02--0.04 of the 0.10 gap (\code{scripts/crmarena\_hindsight\_oracle.py}). Treating the three tiers' outcomes as independent would put the component at 0.10--0.16, but that model predicts a realised oracle of 0.83--0.89, above the observed 0.76, so it is an upper bound rather than an estimate. The reading for the body is that most of the oracle gap on this benchmark is predictable heterogeneity, with the caveat that it rests on one repeated arm; the identical-arms example of Section~\ref{sec:results} shows why the caveat is needed.

\section{A logged episode, step by step}
\label{app:episode}

One task from the B2B counterfactual log, \code{b2b-1755} (category \code{lead\_routing}: which sales user a lead in a given state should be routed to), as the platform recorded it. Every step carries the action as an (op, tier) pair, the propensity the behaviour policy assigned to it, the realised cost and latency, the 43-dimensional feature vector and the legal action set. The mid-tier arm:

{\small\begin{verbatim}
t=0  CONTINUE@mid   propensity 0.333   $0.0141   1.4 s
t=1  CONTINUE@mid   propensity 1.0     $0.0146   1.6 s
t=2  CONTINUE@mid   propensity 1.0     $0.0186   2.9 s
t=3  CONTINUE@mid   propensity 1.0     $0.0172   1.2 s
quality 1.0   total cost $0.0645   latency 7.1 s   end: agent_respond
\end{verbatim}}

\noindent The agent's logged tool calls at those steps: a query for the territory whose description matches the state; a query for the users assigned to that territory; a count of accepted quotes per owner among those users; the answer, one user id, which matched the reference exactly. The propensity is $1/3$ at $t=0$ because the collection's behaviour policy drew the tier uniformly, and $1$ afterwards because the continuation is fixed on the chosen tier (Appendix~\ref{sec:crm}); the controller's decision is the first step only, so later steps carry no weight in any estimator.

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{lrlrr}\toprule
arm & steps & answer & quality & cost \\\midrule
cheap & 7 & a different user id & 0.0 & \$0.049 \\
mid & 4 & correct & 1.0 & \$0.065 \\
frontier & 4 & correct & 1.0 & \$0.128 \\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{The same task on the three arms. The per-task oracle is the mid tier: best quality, cheapest among the correct arms.}
\end{table}

\paragraph{What the controller does with it.} At $t=0$ the feature vector has two non-zero entries, the task category and the harness version. The constrained policy of Section~\ref{sec:crm300} (trained on all 300 tasks, $\bar\lambda=1.09$ per dollar) maps it through a $3\times43$ weight matrix with logit clip $3$ to
\[
\pi(\text{cheap}\mid x)=0.054,\qquad \pi(\text{mid}\mid x)=0.009,\qquad \pi(\text{frontier}\mid x)=0.937 .
\]
It has learned that \code{lead\_routing} tasks, on average over the log, are worth the frontier tier under its budget; on this task that spends twice what the mid tier needed. The gap between the learned policy and the per-task oracle in Table~\ref{tab:crm150} is this gap: the policy conditions on the category, the oracle on the outcome.

\paragraph{What the gate does with it.} On a realised one-arm log that drew the mid tier for this task, the candidate's importance weight is $\pi(\text{mid}\mid x)/(1/3)\approx0.03$, so the episode barely enters the IPS term; a task whose log drew the frontier tier carries weight $2.8$. The doubly-robust estimator (Appendix~\ref{app:estimators}) first predicts the task's quality and cost from its category with a direct model fitted on the other tasks, then adds the weighted residual. Over the 300 tasks this gives quality $0.607$ $[0.525, 0.668]$ and cost $\$0.095$ $[0.087, 0.113]$; the quality lower bound clears $q_{\text{prev}}-\epsilon=0.484$, the cost upper bound does not clear $B+\mathrm{tol}=\$0.105$, and the gate \REVERT{}s. The policy's exact cost on the full log is $\$0.098$, inside the budget by less than the tolerance, which is the case the interval cannot certify at this sample size (Appendix~\ref{sec:gatepower}).

\section{Estimator derivations}
\label{app:estimators}

\paragraph{Unbiasedness of per-decision IPS.} For a logged episode drawn from $\mu$, and any step $t$, $\E_\mu[\rho_{0:t}\,r_t]=\sum_{a_{0:t}}\prod_{k\le t}\mu(a_k\mid s_k)\prod_{k\le t}\frac{\pi(a_k\mid s_k)}{\mu(a_k\mid s_k)}\,r_t=\E_\pi[r_t]$, whenever $\mu(a\mid s)>0$ wherever $\pi(a\mid s)>0$; the $\epsilon$-mixture of Eq.~\eqref{eq:policy} guarantees the support condition for every served log. Summing over $t$ gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:ips}. The clip at $\rho_{\max}$ trades this unbiasedness for variance.

\paragraph{The doubly-robust decomposition.} Write $\delta_t=r_t+\hat V(s_{t+1})-\hat Q(s_t,a_t)$, the temporal-difference residual of the direct model under the candidate. Eq.~\eqref{eq:dr} is $\hat V(s_0)+\sum_t\rho_{0:t}\delta_t$. If $\hat Q=Q^\pi$ then $\E_\mu[\rho_{0:t}\delta_t\mid s_t,a_t]=0$ step by step and the estimate equals $\hat V(s_0)=V^\pi(s_0)$ regardless of the ratios; if instead the ratios are exact (no clip) then telescoping the $\hat V$ and $\hat Q$ terms recovers IPS regardless of $\hat Q$. Either model being right is enough \citep{jiang2016dr}. With a clip, the error is $\sum_t(\rho_{0:t}-\tilde\rho_{0:t})\delta_t$, which is small when the direct model's residuals are small: the clip biases DR only through the direct model, as stated in Section~\ref{sec:problem}.

\paragraph{Bandit DR with cross-fitting.} In Eq.~\eqref{eq:drbandit}, the direct-model term $\sum_a\pi(a\mid x_i)\hat Q^{(-i)}(x_i,a)$ is evaluated on a model that did not see task $i$'s reward, so $\E[w_i(r_i-\hat Q^{(-i)}(x_i,a_i))\mid x_i]$ is the reward model's error under $\pi$, not a term correlated with $r_i$ by construction. With an in-sample model, $\hat Q(x_i,a_i)$ contains $r_i$ itself; with buckets of a few samples the correction term shrinks toward zero and the estimate is pulled toward the direct model. Appendix~\ref{sec:gatepower} reports both choices empirically (coverage 99\%/92\% cross-fitted against 96\%/94\% in-sample with a $-0.006$ quality bias).

\paragraph{Why every arm logged makes the estimators exact.} With three arms per task each at propensity $1/3$, the weights over a task's three episodes are $3\pi(a\mid x)$ and sum to $3\sum_a\pi(a\mid x)=3$; IPS over the 150 episodes is therefore $\frac{1}{50}\sum_i\sum_a\pi(a\mid x_i)r(x_i,a)$, the exact value, and SNIPS and DR coincide with it. The remaining interval is task-sampling uncertainty.

\paragraph{The $\epsilon$-mixture score.} From $\pi_\epsilon=(1-\epsilon)\pi+\epsilon/|\mathcal{A}|$, $\nabla\pi_\epsilon=(1-\epsilon)\nabla\pi=(1-\epsilon)\pi\nabla\log\pi$, so $\nabla\log\pi_\epsilon=\frac{(1-\epsilon)\pi}{\pi_\epsilon}\nabla\log\pi$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:grpo}. Using the bare score would weight exploration draws as if the policy had chosen them.

\paragraph{Effective sample size.} For self-normalised weights, the variance of $\sum_i w_ir_i/\sum_iw_i$ behaves like that of a simple mean over $\mathrm{ESS}=(\sum_iw_i)^2/\sum_iw_i^2$ draws \citep{kish1965survey}; the report flags any estimate with $\mathrm{ESS}<0.1\,n$.

\section{Reproduction}
\label{app:repro}

All offline pipelines are seeded end to end and need only \code{numpy}; the CRMArena-Pro offline script is byte-for-byte deterministic on the committed log. From the repository root:
{\small\begin{verbatim}
pip install -r requirements.txt
bash scripts/run_v0.sh              # sim: collect -> train -> gate -> drift -> retrain -> gate
bash scripts/run_sim_seeds.sh 0 1 2 3 4   # the same pipeline over five replicate seeds
head -150 traces/crmarena_b2b_counterfactual.jsonl > /tmp/b2b_50.jsonl   # the first 50 tasks
python scripts/crmarena_offline.py --log /tmp/b2b_50.jsonl --out results/crmarena   # no API calls
python scripts/crmarena_offline.py --log traces/crmarena_b2b_counterfactual.jsonl \
    --spend traces/crmarena_b2b_counterfactual.spend.jsonl --out results/crmarena_300
python scripts/crmarena_offline.py --log traces/crmarena_b2c_counterfactual.jsonl \
    --spend traces/b2c/spend.jsonl --out results/crmarena_b2c
python -m pace.experiments.taubench.run analyze \
    --log traces/taubench_retail_counterfactual.jsonl --out results/taubench/retail_RESULTS.md
python -m pace.experiments.taubench.run analyze \
    --log traces/taubench_airline_counterfactual.jsonl --out results/taubench/airline_RESULTS.md
python -m pace.experiments.evoharness.run analyze --traces-dir traces/evoharness \
    --out results/evoharness/tools_axis_150.json
python -m pace.experiments.mas.run analyze --log results/mas/mas_b2b.jsonl \
    --single-agent-log traces/crmarena_b2b_counterfactual.jsonl --out results/mas/RESULTS.md
python -m pace.experiments.ssr.run analyze --log traces/ssr/ssr_b2b_tree.jsonl \
    --out results/ssr/RESULTS.md --csv results/ssr/frontier.csv
python -m pace.experiments.mas_continual.run_continual --out results/mas_continual
python -m pace.experiments.mas_continual.run_continual --schedule mild \
    --out results/mas_continual/mild
python -m pace.experiments.mas_continual.analyze --run results/mas_continual \
    --mild results/mas_continual/mild --out results/mas_continual/RESULTS.md
python -m pytest tests -q
\end{verbatim}}
The GPU runs (\code{pace/llm/}) each ran once in one A100-40GB container; their training logs, run metadata, SFT reports and evaluation tables are committed under \code{results/llm/}. The real-harness collections (\code{pace.collect --harness crmarena --mode counterfactual}, the MAS and SSR \code{run} commands) require the benchmark checkout, its sandbox credentials and a model API key, and run under the budget check of Section~\ref{sec:platform}. The figures in this paper are regenerated by \code{python paper/make\_figures.py} from the committed result files, and the paper by \code{make} in \code{paper/}.
